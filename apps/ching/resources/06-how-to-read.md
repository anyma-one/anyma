# How to read a hexagram

A reading has a few layers, and there's a natural order to them. You don't need all of them every time. Start with the big picture, then move to the parts that are specific to your cast.

## 1. Return to your question

Before you read anything, reread what you asked. The text is general, written for everyone who has ever received this hexagram. Your question is what makes it yours. Keep it in view the whole time.

## 2. The hexagram as a whole

**The name** tells you the theme: Beginning, Needing, Retreat, Abundance. Sometimes it's enough on its own.

**The Judgment** belongs to the oldest layer of the book and gives the core verdict on the situation. It's often terse and a little cryptic. Look for its key elements: is this favorable? What does it advise: to act, to wait, to seek help, to hold firm? Is there a condition attached?

**The Image** shows the two trigrams as a scene from nature (thunder under water, a lake on a mountain) and says how to respond to it. Where the Judgment describes the situation, the Image often describes the attitude to bring to it.

**The Meaning** puts both into plain language and draws out what the hexagram asks of you.

**The trigrams** are worth a glance too. The lower one often reflects what's going on within you or at the root of things; the upper one, what's around you or emerging.

## 3. The changing lines

If any of your lines are changing, read their texts next, in order from the bottom up. These are the most specific part of your reading: where the hexagram as a whole describes the situation, a changing line points to your particular place in it.

Traditions differ on how to weigh several changing lines. Some follow rules set down by the scholar Zhu Xi in the 12th century, which, depending on how many lines change, point you to one line, a pair of lines, or the Judgments instead; others read them all. This app shows you all of them. If they seem to pull in different directions, that's information too: the situation may have more than one moving part.

## 4. The second hexagram

Changing lines transform your hexagram into a second one. A common way to read the pair: the first hexagram is where you are now; the second is where the change is taking you, or what the situation is becoming. Read its name, Judgment and Meaning, but more lightly. It's a direction, not a destination.

## Special cases

**No changing lines.** About one cast in six. The situation is stable, a single picture rather than a story in motion. The whole message lives in the one hexagram.

**All six changing.** About one cast in four thousand. Everything is in motion; tradition reads this mostly through the second hexagram. Hexagrams 1 and 2 have a special extra text for this case.

## The six positions

Each hexagram tells its theme as a story in six stages, rising from the bottom. Once you know the pattern, you'll see it everywhere.

| Line | Position | Typical character |
|---|---|---|
| 6 | Top | Beyond the peak. Too late, too far, overripe; sometimes the wisdom of withdrawal. |
| 5 | Ruler | The center of the upper trigram and the place of authority. Usually the strongest position. |
| 4 | Minister | Close to power but not holding it. Needs care, tact and loyalty. |
| 3 | Threshold | The top of the lower trigram, crossing into the upper. Often unsettled or dangerous. |
| 2 | Inner center | The center of the lower trigram. Usually favorable: grounded, balanced, quietly effective. |
| 1 | Beginning | The situation just starting. Often too early to act fully; a time to set foundations. |

These are tendencies, not rules. The line texts sometimes break them, and those surprises are worth noticing.

Two more patterns the tradition uses: lines 1 and 4, 2 and 5, and 3 and 6 "correspond," and a firm line paired with a yielding one in these positions is a good sign. And odd positions (1, 3, 5) suit solid lines, even positions (2, 4, 6) suit broken ones. You don't need these to read well, but you'll see older commentaries use them constantly.

## Reading well

- **Look for the stance, not the prediction.** The I Ching rarely says what will happen. It says how to meet what's happening.
- **Notice your first reaction.** Relief, resistance, recognition: your response often tells you more than the text.
- **Write it down.** Note the date, your question, the hexagram and changing lines, and what you made of it. Rereading old casts months later is one of the best ways to learn.
- **Don't force it.** If a reading doesn't fit yet, leave it. Sometimes the connection arrives later.
