# On translation

Every English I Ching is an interpretation. That's not a flaw to apologize for; it's one of the most interesting things about the book. Seeing how translators differ teaches you more about the hexagrams than any single version can.

## Gua, not just "hexagram"

The Chinese word for these figures is *gua* (卦). "Hexagram" is a Western coinage: Greek for "six marks." *Gua* is broader. It covers both the six-line figures and the three-line trigrams, and its root sense is a divination sign, something laid out to be read. This app says "hexagram" for clarity, but when you read Chinese sources or older practitioners, *gua* is the word you'll meet.

## Names: verbs or things?

Look at how two well-known translators name the same figures:

| No. | Chinese | Alfred Huang | Richard Wilhelm |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Qián 乾 | Initiating | The Creative |
| 2 | Kūn 坤 | Responding | The Receptive |
| 24 | Fù 復 | Turning Back | Return |
| 29 | Kǎn 坎 | Darkness | The Abysmal |
| 49 | Gé 革 | Abolishing the Old | Revolution |
| 50 | Dǐng 鼎 | Establishing the New | The Caldron |

Wilhelm tends toward nouns and archetypes: The Creative, The Receptive, The Caldron. Huang tends toward verbs and processes: Initiating, Responding, Establishing the New.

Neither is wrong. Classical Chinese doesn't mark whether a word is a noun or a verb; the same character can be either, depending on how you read it. So every translator has to choose, and the choice reveals a way of seeing. Wilhelm's names suggest timeless principles. Huang's names suggest movement, which suits a book whose very title means *change*.

Huang isn't perfectly consistent, either: Multitude, Union, Household and Abundance are nouns. And sometimes the two agree completely, as with Keeping Still (52). This app uses Huang's names as the main ones and shows Wilhelm's as a reference, because many English readers already know Wilhelm's.

## One character, many meanings

Hexagram 50 shows how much a single character can hold. Dǐng (鼎) is a bronze cauldron used for cooking and for offerings to ancestors. Owning the great ritual cauldrons was a symbol of a dynasty's right to rule. So the hexagram is about nourishment, about sacred vessels, and about founding a lasting order, all at once. Wilhelm keeps the object ("The Caldron"). Huang names what it stands for ("Establishing the New"). Each gains something and loses something.

Hexagram 29, Kǎn (坎), means a pit or hollow, and as a trigram it stands for water. Wilhelm's "The Abysmal" keeps the depth; Huang's "Darkness" keeps the mood. A third translator might say "The Pit" or "Danger." All of these are inside the character.

## Spellings change too

James Legge's 1882 translation names Hexagram 1 "Khien" and Hexagram 3 "Kun." These are older ways of writing Chinese sounds in the Latin alphabet. Today's standard is pinyin, which writes the same names as Qián and Zhūn. Same hexagrams, different spelling systems.

Pinyin has its own trap: several different hexagrams share a spelling and differ only in tone. Qián (1) and Qiān (15) are different words, as are Kūn (2) and Kùn (47). That's why this app always shows the tone marks and the character.

## The "superior person"

Many Image texts describe what *jūnzǐ* (君子) does in response to the scene. Literally, the phrase means "a lord's son," a nobleman. Confucius turned it into a moral ideal: a person of noble character, whatever their birth. Legge and Wilhelm both translate it "the superior man." Modern versions try "the noble one," "the wise person" or "the exemplary person."

This app makes a different choice and says "you." The Image is advice addressed to whoever is reading, and the old phrase, however well meant, puts a distance between the reader and the advice. It's a translation decision like any other, and you're free to disagree with it.

## What this app's texts are

The texts in this app are original paraphrases, written in modern English and checked for meaning against Legge's public-domain translation. They're not word-for-word translations of the Chinese. Where translators disagree about a passage, they generally follow the mainstream reading shared by Wilhelm and others. If you want to go deeper, reading Legge, Wilhelm and Huang side by side is the best next step: where all three agree, you're on firm ground; where they differ, you've found something worth thinking about.
