# A brief history of the I Ching

The I Ching, or *Yijing* (易經), means "the Classic of Changes." It is one of the oldest books still in use anywhere, and for most of its life it has been two things at once: a manual for divination and a book of philosophy. Both matter for understanding how to use it well.

## Before the book: cracks in bone

Long before the I Ching existed, the kings of the Shang dynasty (roughly 1600 to 1046 BCE) consulted their ancestors by heating ox bones and turtle shells until they cracked, then reading the cracks. Specialists recorded the question and the answer on the bone itself. More than a hundred thousand fragments of these "oracle bones" survive, and they contain the earliest Chinese writing we have.

The Zhou, who overthrew the Shang, came to favor a different method. Alongside fire, they used bundles of yarrow stalks, sorted and counted to produce numbers, and the numbers produced lines. That system of lines, and the texts written to interpret them, became the *Zhouyi*: the Changes of Zhou.

## The core text

The core of the I Ching is short: 64 figures of six lines each, a brief statement for each figure (the Judgment), and a brief statement for each of its six lines. That's all. Everything else came later.

Tradition gives this text a heroic origin. The legendary sage Fu Xi is said to have discovered the eight trigrams by observing the patterns of nature. King Wen, whose son would found the Zhou dynasty, is said to have arranged the 64 hexagrams and written the Judgments while imprisoned by the last Shang king. His son, the Duke of Zhou, is said to have written the line statements.

Modern scholars see it differently. The core text was most likely compiled during the Western Zhou, around the 9th century BCE, from older divination records and sayings. That's why so many lines refer to concrete things: hunts, river crossings, marriages, sacrifices, military campaigns. They read like notes from real consultations, polished into a reference work.

## The Ten Wings

Over the following centuries, a set of commentaries grew around the core text. Tradition attributes them to Confucius; scholars date them to roughly the 4th to 2nd centuries BCE. They are called the Ten Wings, and they did something remarkable: they turned a diviner's handbook into a work of philosophy. The Image texts you read in this app come from one of these Wings. So does the idea that the 64 hexagrams describe the patterns of change running through all of nature and human life.

## How it was actually used

Historical records, above all the *Zuo zhuan* (a chronicle of the 8th to 5th centuries BCE), describe consultations of the Changes for weighty matters: whether to go to war, whether a marriage alliance would prosper, what the future held for a ruler's newborn heir. These were rare events, prepared for carefully and taken seriously.

Even then, the wisest users treated the text as a prompt for judgment rather than a replacement for it. The philosopher Xunzi (3rd century BCE) put it in a single line: those who truly understand the Changes do not need to divine. The point of practice was to absorb the book's way of seeing until you could read situations without casting at all.

## Survival and authority

When the first emperor of Qin ordered the burning of books in 213 BCE, the I Ching was spared because it was classed as a practical divination manual. Under the Han dynasty it became one of the Five Classics, the core of the education every scholar and official received, and it stayed there for two thousand years.

Later thinkers kept reinterpreting it. Wang Bi (3rd century CE) read it as philosophy and set aside much of the numerology. Shao Yong (11th century) arranged the hexagrams into a perfect binary sequence. Zhu Xi (12th century) restored its role as a divination text and wrote down rules for casting that many people still follow.

In 1973, archaeologists opening a tomb at Mawangdui, sealed in 168 BCE, found the oldest complete copy of the text, written on silk. Its hexagrams are arranged in a completely different order from the received version, a reminder that even the book's structure has a history.

## Arrival in the West

Jesuit missionaries brought the I Ching to Europe in the 17th century. In 1703 the philosopher Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz noticed that its solid and broken lines matched the binary numbers he had been working with. James Legge published a scholarly English translation in 1882. Richard Wilhelm's German translation of 1924, put into English by Cary F. Baynes in 1950 with a foreword by Carl Jung, became the version most Western readers know. Alfred Huang's *The Complete I Ching* (1998), whose names this app uses, brought a native Chinese perspective to the English-speaking world.

## Why this matters for you

Three things from this history shape how this app teaches:

1. **It was consulted rarely, for things that mattered.** Not as a daily habit.
2. **It was always read by a person.** The text is general; the asker applies it. That's the skill you're learning.
3. **It has been reinterpreted constantly.** There is no single correct reading of any hexagram, which is why we show you more than one translation of the names, and why your own reading counts.
