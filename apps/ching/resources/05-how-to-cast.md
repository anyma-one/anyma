# How to cast with coins

Casting builds a hexagram one line at a time, from the bottom up, with three coins. It takes about five minutes. The method is simple; what takes practice is the attention you bring to it.

## What you need

- Three coins of the same kind. Any coins work.
- Something to write with.
- A question you've taken a moment to think about. (See "Asking a good question" below.)

## The steps

**1. Decide which side counts as what.** The usual convention: heads count 3, tails count 2. Some traditions reverse this. Either is fine, as long as you stay with one.

**2. Toss all three coins and add them up.** The total is always 6, 7, 8 or 9.

| Coins | Total | Line | Draw it as |
|---|---|---|---|
| Three tails | 6 | Old yin | ⚋ broken, marked as changing (often with an ×) |
| Two tails, one head | 7 | Young yang | ⚊ solid |
| Two heads, one tail | 8 | Young yin | ⚋ broken |
| Three heads | 9 | Old yang | ⚊ solid, marked as changing (often with a ○) |

**3. Draw the line at the bottom.** Your first toss is line 1, the lowest line of the hexagram.

**4. Repeat five more times,** drawing each new line above the last. After six tosses you have a hexagram.

**5. Find your hexagram.** Split it into two trigrams: lines 1 to 3 are the lower trigram, lines 4 to 6 the upper. Look up the pair in the hexagram table.

**6. If any lines are changing, find your second hexagram.** Redraw the hexagram, flipping every changing line into its opposite: old yin becomes solid, old yang becomes broken. Leave the stable lines as they are. The new figure is your second hexagram, showing where the situation is heading.

## Why some results are rarer than others

You might expect each of the four line types to come up a quarter of the time. They don't. Three tails can happen only one way, and so can three heads. But a mixed result can happen three ways: the odd coin out can be the first, the second or the third. So:

- Old yin (6): 1 in 8
- Young yang (7): 3 in 8
- Young yin (8): 3 in 8
- Old yang (9): 1 in 8

Each line has a 1 in 4 chance of changing. Most casts have one or two changing lines. About one cast in six has none, and all six changing happens about once in four thousand casts.

This matters. If a program picked line types at random with equal odds, changing lines would come up twice as often as they should, and readings would be full of movement that the real method doesn't produce. This app simulates three real coins for exactly that reason.

## The older method: yarrow stalks

The original method used 50 yarrow stalks, divided and counted in a slow ritual that takes twenty minutes or more. It also produces 6, 7, 8 and 9, but with different odds: old yin 1 in 16, young yang 5 in 16, young yin 7 in 16, old yang 3 in 16. Notice that the yarrow method is lopsided: old yang comes up three times as often as old yin, so solid lines change far more readily than broken ones. The coin method, a later shortcut, evens this out. Some practitioners prefer yarrow because of this imbalance, and for its slowness, which gives the question time to settle.

## Asking a good question

The I Ching doesn't answer yes or no. It describes the situation you're in and the stance it asks of you. So open questions work best:

- Instead of "Will I get the job?", ask "What should I understand about this job search?"
- Instead of "Should I leave?", ask "What do I need to see about staying or leaving?"

Write your question down before you cast, so you can't quietly change it afterwards to fit the answer.

## Casting again

Tradition is strict about this: asking the same question again because you didn't like the answer means you've stopped listening. Hexagram 4 says so plainly. But practice is different. If you're learning the method, cast as often as you like; repetition is how the skill becomes yours. The line to watch is your motive: practicing, or shopping for a better answer?
